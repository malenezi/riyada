# Sizing methods and sources

## Three ways to size, and when to use each

| Method | How | Use when | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bottom-up | Count customer units, multiply by fit and annual value | Always, as the primary estimate | Needs a reliable count of units |
| Top-down | Start from a published total, cut by segment shares | As a cross-check | Shares are usually guesses; totals are often broader than your market |
| Value-based | Cost of the problem per customer x customers x share you can capture as price | New categories with no existing spend | Depends on a credible cost-of-problem figure |

If bottom-up and top-down differ by more than about three times, one of them is defining the market differently. Find out which before quoting either.

## The three levels

- **TAM**: everyone who has the problem, at your price. Useful for showing headroom, not for planning.
- **SAM**: the part you can serve with this product, language, licence and geography.
- **SOM**: what your channels can win in a stated period. Compute it from capacity, not from a percentage:

  `SOM customers = qualified leads per month x close rate x months - expected churn`

A plan built on SOM should survive halving the close rate.

## Where inputs come from

Search for these source types before anything else, and record the date of the data, not the date you read it.

| Input | Source types, in order of preference |
|---|---|
| Number of businesses or households | National statistics office, commercial registry or licensing data, chamber of commerce, municipal open data |
| Sector revenue or spend | Regulator and central bank reports, listed-company annual reports, trade associations |
| Prices | Vendors' public price pages, tenders and procurement portals, customer interviews |
| Adoption and behaviour | Government surveys, academic studies, the founder's own interviews and pilots |
| Competitor scale | Company filings, funding announcements from the company itself, job postings as a rough signal |

Treat with caution: vendor "market report" summaries with no method, figures repeated across blogs without an original source, and anything undated. If the only source is of this kind, say so beside the number.

## Confidence labels

- **High**: primary source, dated within two years, definition matches yours.
- **Medium**: reputable secondary source, or a primary source with a definition that needs adjusting.
- **Low**: assumption, extrapolation, or a single unverified source.

Report the overall estimate at the confidence of its weakest multiplied input.

## Common errors

- Counting companies when the buying unit is a branch or a seat, or the reverse.
- Mixing years: 2022 unit counts with 2026 prices.
- Double counting segments that overlap.
- Treating list price as realised price; apply typical discounts.
- Ignoring the "do nothing" alternative, which holds most of the market in new categories.
- Converting currencies without stating the rate and date.

## Competitor research checklist

1. List what buyers named in interviews first; search second.
2. For each alternative record: target customer, price as published, date checked, link.
3. Identify the buyer's top three criteria from interviews or lost deals. Compare on those.
4. Mark unknown cells; plan how to verify each (trial account, reference call, public filing).
5. Note where an alternative is the better choice. That segment is not yours.
